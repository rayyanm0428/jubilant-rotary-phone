HISTORY & CONTEXT

Explain key moments in the historical development of AI and ML and relate them to modern AI systems.

The 1973 Lighthill Report marked the moment AI had to answer for its promises, and it only makes sense in light of how the field got there. After researchers at the 1956 Dartmouth workshop named the field, early programs like the Logic Theorist and ELIZA produced impressive results by representing knowledge as symbols and rules, and optimism ran far ahead of what the systems could do. By the early 1970s these systems could not handle real-world complexity, computing power was far too limited, and demos worked only on narrow, curated examples. The Lighthill Report told the UK government that AI had failed to deliver, and funding collapsed on both sides of the Atlantic, which began the first AI winter. This was a significant historical moment because it showed that technical progress and public expectations can drift apart, and that the whole field pays for the gap. Since then the pattern has repeated and evolved. AI recovered in the 1980s through expert systems like MYCIN, which captured a human expert’s rules for one domain, but they proved brittle outside those rules and a second winter followed. That failure pushed researchers toward learning from data, which led to the deep learning breakthrough of 2012 and the generative systems of today. I see the same lesson in self-driving cars. In the mid-2010s, autonomous vehicles handled highways and mapped routes so smoothly that many companies predicted driverless cars within a few years, and then rare situations like unusual construction zones or a person directing traffic by hand exposed how narrow that success was. These systems learn from data rather than hand-written rules, so the lesson is not that rules were the only problem. Strong performance on typical cases does not guarantee strong performance on unusual ones, and I now judge any AI demo by whether it shows the hard cases.

ETHICS & SOCIETY

Identify and discuss ethical issues associated with AI and ML and their societal implications.

Facial recognition in policing shows how an AI system can work and still cause serious harm. In 2020, Robert Williams was wrongfully arrested in Detroit after a facial recognition match misidentified him as a shoplifting suspect. The event touches all three ways a working system can go wrong. On fairness, algorithmic bias is well documented in these systems, and a 2019 federal study found higher false match rates for Black and Asian faces than for white faces, so the same tool applied to everyone produces different error rates for different groups. On calibrated trust, a match is only a lead, but it carries the authority of a computer output, and the system does not signal how confident it is or when it is likely to be wrong. That is how a weak guess can be treated as strong evidence. On misuse, the dual-use problem appears because a tool built to find suspects can also track protesters or ordinary people in public spaces. The regulation gap resembles the one seen with hiring audits, where a law on paper is not the same as a law that works. Some cities and states have restricted police use while no comprehensive federal law exists, so protection depends on where someone lives. The societal implications fall hardest on people who are already most likely to be stopped, and a wrongful arrest can cost someone their job, their reputation, and their trust in public institutions. What I take from this is that average accuracy is the wrong measure. A system can be accurate overall and still fail the people least able to absorb the failure. Applying fairness, calibrated trust, and dual use to a new setting showed me they work as a general framework, and they belong at the start of design rather than after harm occurs.
