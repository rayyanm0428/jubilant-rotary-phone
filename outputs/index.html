HISTORY & CONTEXT

Explain key moments in the historical development of AI and ML and relate them to modern AI systems.

The AI winters and the brittleness of hand-written rules (1970s to the 1990s). In the early 1970s, AI systems couldn’t handle real-world complexity, and early demos only worked on narrow, curated examples. After the 1973 Lighthill Report concluded that AI had failed to deliver on its promises, funding collapsed. AI came back in the 1980s with expert systems like MYCIN, which captured a human expert’s rules for one specific domain and matched human specialists in evaluations. But these systems were brittle, and they broke down outside their narrow rule sets and became expensive to maintain as the rules multiplied, which led to the second winter and pushed researchers toward learning from data. What I take from this is a pattern. A system looks great in a controlled setting, people assume it will keep working as conditions get messier, and then rare, unexpected cases expose how narrow it really was. Self-driving cars are a good example from outside the slides. In the mid-2010s, autonomous vehicles looked close to solved because they handled highways and mapped routes smoothly, and many companies predicted fully driverless cars within a few years. But driving is full of rare situations, like odd construction zones, unusual weather, or a person directing traffic by hand, and a system that excelled at common cases could fail on those. Self-driving systems learn from data instead of following hand-written rules, so the lesson isn’t that rules were the only problem. It’s that doing well on typical examples doesn’t guarantee doing well on unusual ones, which is why I now ask of any AI product whether its demo shows how it handles the hard, rare cases or only the easy ones.

ETHICS & SOCIETY

Identify and discuss ethical issues associated with AI and ML and their societal implications.

An AI system can do exactly what it was built to do and still cause harm, and from what I have studied this usually happens in three ways. It can treat groups of people differently even when the same rule is applied to everyone. It can give advice that sounds confident in situations where it should not be trusted, and it never signals which situation you are in. And it can be turned toward a purpose its creators never had in mind. Behind all three sits a gap between how fast the technology spreads and how slowly rules and oversight catch up, so the people affected often take the hit before anyone is held responsible. I wanted to see whether these ideas hold up outside the examples from class, so I looked at AI-powered exam proctoring software. These tools use a webcam and algorithms to flag students who look away from the screen, move around a lot, or have odd lighting or background noise. On fairness, the rule is the same for every student but the results are not. A student with a disability that affects eye movement, a student sharing a small space with family, or a student whose skin tone the camera reads poorly could all be flagged far more often. This is a proxy problem, where a signal that looks neutral, like how often someone glances away, ends up standing in for things it was never meant to measure. On trust, a “suspicious behavior” flag looks authoritative, but the system usually cannot explain why it flagged someone or how sure it is, so a reviewer may treat a weak guess as strong evidence, and nothing in the output says whether the case was obvious or borderline. On misuse, a tool built to protect academic integrity also records video inside students’ homes, and that footage could be kept, shared, or reused for surveillance well beyond the exam. The regulation gap shows up here too, since schools often adopt these tools without any requirement to test them for bias first. Working through this example gave me a way of thinking that I can apply to any AI system that affects people. It means asking who could end up with a different outcome from the same rule, whether the system signals when its output should not be trusted, what else the technology could be used for, and whether anyone is actually checking how it behaves. For me, this changes AI ethics from a question of whether a system works into a broader question of who it works for, who pays when it fails, and who is responsible for finding out.
